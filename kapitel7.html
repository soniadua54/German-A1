KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 7 introduces the third and final basic case you need at A1: the dative. It shows up in personal pronouns ("to me," "to him") and in prepositions describing location. This chapter is dense — go slowly.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn office and workplace vocabulary, the dative case for articles and personal pronouns, dative prepositions of place (bei, mit, nach, von, zu), how to connect sentences with und, oder, aber, and how to write a simple formal email.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Describe a typical workday and small tasks at the office
  • Use dative pronouns: "give it to me," "it suits her," "I'll help him"
  • Say where you are with dative prepositions: at the doctor's, with a colleague, to the bank
  • Connect ideas smoothly with and/or/but
  • Write and understand a short formal email

Where you use this in real life

Describing your workday at BRPL, writing a polite email to a colleague, saying something suits or doesn't suit you (shopping, decisions), or explaining you're going to the doctor's or the bank.

GrammatikGrammar

Third case: the dative, used for the indirect object ("to/for whom") and after certain fixed prepositions.

1. What the dative marks the indirect object — "to/for someone"

Where accusative marks the direct object (the thing acted on), dative marks the indirect object — the person something is given, said, or done to/for. English shows this with "to" or "for," or by word order ("I give him the book" = "I give the book to him").

✓
Ich gebe ihm das Buch.
I give him the book. (ihm = dative, "to him")

Common mistake

Certain verbs (helfen, gefallen, passen, gehören) always take a dative object even when English would use a direct object — "help me" looks like a direct object in English, but German helfen demands dative: hilf mir, not hilf mich.

3. Dative articles der/die/das change again

GenderNominativeDative

Memory trick

Dative masculine and neuter both become dem — they merge into one form, unlike accusative where only masculine changes. Feminine becomes der (yes, it borrows the word that used to mean masculine "the" — this is a genuine source of confusion, so just memorize it as its own fact). Plural becomes den plus an -n on the noun itself if it doesn't already end in one.

Contractions to memorize

zu + dem → zum, zu + der → zur, bei + dem → beim, von + dem → vom. These contractions are used constantly in speech — learning the fused forms directly (rather than the two separate words) will make you sound far more natural.

5. Connecting sentences: und, oder, aber

These three connectors don't change word order — they simply link two full sentences, each keeping its own normal verb-second structure.

✓
Ich arbeite im Büro und ich beantworte E-Mails.
I work in the office and I answer emails.
✓
Ich kann heute kommen, aber ich muss um vier gehen.
I can come today, but I have to leave at four.
✓
Willst du Kaffee oder Tee?
Do you want coffee or tea?

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Kannst du mich helfen?
Kannst du mir helfen?
Why: helfen is one of a fixed group of German verbs that always take a dative object, even though the English equivalent "help me" looks like a direct object.
Ich gehe zu der Arzt.
Ich gehe zum Arzt.
Why: zu + dem contracts to zum in everyday speech — using the uncontracted form sounds stilted and is generally avoided.
Das gefällt ich.
Das gefällt mir.
Why: gefallen takes a dative pronoun for the person who likes something — never the nominative subject pronoun.
Ich arbeite im Büro, ich beantworte E-Mails.
Ich arbeite im Büro und ich beantworte E-Mails.
Why: German, like English, needs a connector such as und between two full sentences — simply placing them side by side with a comma is a run-on, not a joined sentence.

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

This is the payoff chapter for the "Learn Later" notes back in Kapitel 6: the Perfekt tense, the real everyday past tense for almost every German verb. Once you have this, you can talk about anything that already happened.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn to describe your daily routine and past events using the Perfekt (compound past with haben or sein + past participle), talk about job searching and studies, and handle a phone call professionally.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Describe what you did yesterday or last week
  • Form the past participle of regular and common irregular verbs
  • Know when to use haben vs. sein as the helper verb
  • Talk about job searching, studies, and qualifications
  • Handle a basic phone call, including asking someone to repeat something

Where you use this in real life

Recounting your day to Sonia, describing a work project you finished, explaining your study background in an interview, or narrating a weekend activity beyond just war/hatte.

GrammatikGrammar

The main event: the Perfekt tense. This is the tense native speakers reach for by default when talking about the past in conversation.

1. What Perfekt is a two-part past tense

Perfekt is built from two pieces: a helper verb (haben or sein, conjugated normally in the present tense) plus the past participle of the main verb, which is pushed to the very end of the sentence — the same "verb bracket" shape you saw with modal verbs in Kapitel 5.

[subject] + haben/sein (conjugated) + [rest] + [past participle].
Ichsubject
habehelper — pos. 2

Learn later

Irregular participles follow patterns (vowel changes) that get fully systematized in A2. For now, memorize each one individually as vocabulary — treat essen → gegessen as a fixed fact, not something to derive from a rule.

4. Haben or sein? choosing the right helper verb

Most verbs use haben. A smaller group — mostly verbs of motion (going somewhere) or change of state — use sein instead.

InfinitiveParticipleHelper
gehengegangensein
fahrengefahrensein
kommengekommensein
machengemachthaben
arbeitengearbeitethaben
✓
Ich bin ins Büro gegangen.
I went to the office. (motion → sein)
✓
Ich habe eine Präsentation gemacht.
I gave a presentation. (not motion → haben)
✕
Ich habe ins Büro gegangen.
Wrong — gehen is a motion verb and takes sein, not haben

Memory trick

A rough (not perfect, but useful at A1) rule: if you can picture the subject physically moving from A to B, or changing state (like werden — to become), it's probably sein. Everything else defaults to haben. sein and bleiben (to stay) are themselves oddball exceptions that also take sein despite not being motion verbs — just memorize those two as extras.

5. Separable verbs in Perfekt the ge- lands in the middle

For separable verbs (like einkaufen), the ge- of the participle is inserted between the prefix and the stem.

✓
Ich habe eingekauft.
I went shopping. (ein + ge + kauft)
✓
Ich bin aufgestanden.
I got up. (auf + ge + standen)

AussprachePronunciation

ge- (unstressed)
The ge- prefix of past participles is always unstressed — the emphasis falls on the verb stem that follows it.
gemacht (stress on -MACHT) · gegangen (stress on -GAN-)
-iert (studiert)
Verbs borrowed from other languages (often ending -ieren) form their participle with just -t, no ge- prefix, and the stress falls on the -ier- syllable.
studiert · telefoniert
ei (Arbeit)
"Eye" sound, as established since Kapitel 1.
Arbeit · gearbeitet

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Ich habe ins Büro gegangen.
Ich bin ins Büro gegangen.
Why: gehen is a motion verb and belongs to the smaller group that takes sein as the helper, not haben.
Ich habe gegessen das Brot.
Ich habe das Brot gegessen.
Why: the past participle must go all the way to the end of the sentence — this is the same verb-bracket rule you saw with modal verbs.
Ich bin ein Buch gelesen.
Ich habe ein Buch gelesen.
Why: lesen doesn't involve motion or a change of state, so it defaults to haben, not sein.

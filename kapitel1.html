Guten Tag! — Hello / Good day

Greetings, introductions, and the words that open every conversation

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 1 is where you learn to open your mouth in German without freezing. It's short on grammar and long on survival phrases — the goal is fluency in a narrow, extremely common situation: meeting someone.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn to greet people appropriately for time of day and formality, say who you are, ask who someone else is, say where you're from and what language(s) you speak, and count from 0–20 (needed for phone numbers and basic exchanges). Grammatically, the whole chapter rests on one verb: sein ("to be") in the ich / du / Sie / er / sie forms, plus the very first taste of German word order.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Greet someone correctly at any time of day, formally or informally
  • Introduce yourself: name, origin, language(s)
  • Ask someone else the same things, both formally (Sie) and informally (du)
  • Spell your name using the German alphabet
  • Say goodbye appropriately
  • Count 0–20 and exchange a phone number

Where you use this in real life

Literally the first 30 seconds of any interaction in German: at the door of a language school, at a reception desk, meeting a neighbor, on the phone, at the start of a class. If you only ever learned this chapter, you could survive a first meeting — badly, but you'd survive it.

GrammatikGrammar

Three things: the verb sein, subject pronouns, and the skeleton of a German sentence (verb in position two — this rule will run your whole German life, so get it early).

1. Personal pronouns who's talking

German has a formality split English doesn't: du (informal "you," one person) vs. Sie (formal "you," always capitalized, singular or plural). Getting this wrong isn't a small mistake — calling a stranger or your boss du can sound rude or overly familiar.

GermanEnglishUsed for
ichIyourself
duyou (informal)friends, family, children, other students
Sieyou (formal)strangers, teachers, officials, anyone older/senior — always capital S
er / sie / eshe / she / ittalking about someone or something
wirweyourself + others
ihryou all (informal)a group of friends
sietheylowercase — context tells you it's not "Sie"

Memory trick

If you'd shake their hand and use a title (Herr/Frau + last name) → Sie. If you'd hug them or use their first name → du. When in real doubt in Germany, start with Sie — going formal-to-informal is safe, the reverse is awkward.

2. The verb sein "to be" — irregular, memorize it now

Like English "to be," German sein is irregular — it doesn't follow the normal verb pattern you'll learn later. You must simply learn these forms by heart; you'll use them constantly.

PronounFormExample
ichbinIch bin Aman.
dubistDu bist müde.
SiesindSie sind Frau Klein.
er/sie/esistSie ist aus Berlin.
wirsindWir sind hier.
ihrseidIhr seid spät.
sie (they)sindSie sind Kollegen.

Memory trick

Sie, wir, and sie (they) all take sind — three different subjects, one shared form. Learn that cluster together.

3. Word order: verb in position two the rule that runs everything

In a normal statement, the conjugated verb is always the second element — not necessarily the second word, but the second "slot." The subject usually comes first, but it doesn't have to.

Position 1 (any element) — VERB — rest of sentence
Ichpos. 1
binverb — pos. 2
Aman.rest

Now watch what happens when you move something else to position one — the verb stays in position two, and the subject slides after it. This is different from English, where the subject is glued to the front.

Heutepos. 1
binverb — pos. 2
ichsubject moves
müde.rest
✓
Ich bin aus Indien.
I am from India.
✓
Aus Indien bin ich.
From India, I am. (verb still position 2 — sounds a little poetic/emphatic, but grammatically correct)
✕
Aus Indien ich bin.
Wrong — verb has drifted to position three

Common mistake

English speakers default to Subject–Verb–Object no matter what, so when a time word or place word starts the sentence, they forget to move the verb up. Heute ich bin müde is the literal English order translated — and it's wrong. The verb must stay glued to position two.

4. Yes/No questions: verb moves to position one flipping a statement into a question

VERB — subject — rest — ?
✓
Sind Sie Frau Meyer?
Are you Ms. Meyer?
✓
Bist du müde?
Are you tired?

5. W-questions "question word" questions

Question word — VERB — subject — rest — ?
✓
Wie heißen Sie?
What is your name? (literally: "how are you called")
✓
Woher kommst du?
Where are you from?

Notice: the verb is still in position two, right after the question word. The rule never breaks — it's the same skeleton as a statement, just with a question word occupying position one instead of the subject.

6. Countries after "aus" most take no article — four exceptions

Almost every country drops its article after aus ("from"): aus Spanien, aus Japan, aus Brasilien. Four countries are exceptions and keep an article:

✓
aus der Schweiz
from Switzerland
✓
aus der Türkei
from Turkey
✓
aus der Ukraine
from Ukraine
✓
aus den USA
from the USA

Memory trick

At A1, don't worry about why — just memorize these four as fixed phrases, exactly as your Kursbuch presents them (page 15). The reasoning (these four are grammatically feminine or plural, unlike most country names) becomes relevant much later once you learn the dative case in full.

7. Personalpronomen in Texten using er/sie instead of repeating a name

Once you've named someone, switch to er (for a man) or sie (for a woman) in the sentences that follow — this is exactly how your Kursbuch writes short bios, and it's the skill behind writing any short paragraph about a person.

✓
Das ist Frau Lang. Sie kommt aus Deutschland. Sie wohnt in Frankfurt.
This is Ms. Lang. She's from Germany. She lives in Frankfurt.
✓
Das ist Jan. Er kommt aus Frankfurt. Er wohnt in Zürich.
This is Jan. He's from Frankfurt. He lives in Zürich.

SatzfabrikSentence factory

One pattern, many real sentences. Watch how swapping a single slot changes the whole meaning — this is how you stop translating and start building.

Base pattern: Ich bin ... / Ich komme aus ... / Ich spreche ...

ich + bin/komme/spreche + [name / place / language]
SubjectVerbRestFull sentence
IchbinAman.Ich bin Aman.
Ichkommeaus Indien.Ich komme aus Indien.
IchsprecheDeutsch und Englisch.Ich spreche Deutsch und Englisch.
Ichwohnein Delhi.Ich wohne in Delhi.

Swap the subject — same skeleton, new person

✓
Sonia kommt aus Deutschland.
Sonia is from Germany.
✓
Er ist Lehrer.
He is a teacher.
✓
Wir kommen aus Noida.
We're from Noida.

Watch the verb ending

The subject changes → the verb ending changes with it: ich komme, er/sie kommt, wir kommen. This is verb conjugation — you'll get the full regular pattern in Kapitel 2, but notice it starting here.

Swap the time or place slot — move it to position one

✓
In Delhi wohne ich.
In Delhi is where I live. (place fronted for emphasis)
✓
Heute bin ich müde.
Today I'm tired.

Chain sentences with und

✓
Ich heiße Aman und ich komme aus Indien.
My name is Aman and I'm from India.
✓
Sie ist aus Berlin und sie spricht Deutsch und Englisch.
She's from Berlin and she speaks German and English.

FragefabrikQuestion factory

Kapitel 1 only needs four question words plus yes/no questions. Master the pattern below and you can ask a stranger everything you need at a first meeting.

Wie how

✓
Wie heißen Sie? — Ich heiße Aman Bhatt.
What's your name? — My name is Aman Bhatt.
✓
Wie geht es Ihnen? — Danke, gut.
How are you (formal)? — Fine, thanks.
✓
Wie geht's? — Gut, danke, und dir?
How's it going (informal)? — Good, thanks, and you?

Woher where...from

✓
Woher kommen Sie? — Ich komme aus Indien.
Where are you from? — I'm from India.
✓
Woher kommst du? — Aus Delhi.
Where are you from (informal)? — From Delhi. (short answer)

Wo where (location, not origin)

✓
Wo wohnen Sie? — Ich wohne in Noida.
Where do you live? — I live in Noida.

Common mistake

Wo and woher are not interchangeable. Wo = where (currently located). Woher = where from (origin). "Woher wohnen Sie?" is wrong — you don't "live from" somewhere.

Was what

✓
Was sind Sie von Beruf? — Ich bin Ingenieur.
What's your profession? — I'm an engineer.

Yes/No questions

✓
Kommen Sie aus Deutschland? — Nein, ich komme aus Indien.
Are you from Germany? — No, I'm from India.
✓
Sprichst du Englisch? — Ja, ein bisschen.
Do you speak English? — Yes, a little.

Short answers vs. full answers

QuestionShort answerFull answer
Woher kommst du?Aus Indien.Ich komme aus Indien.
Wie heißt du?Aman.Ich heiße Aman.
Sprechen Sie Deutsch?Ja, ein bisschen.Ja, ich spreche ein bisschen Deutsch.

Native speakers use short answers constantly in casual speech — full answers everywhere sound stiff and textbook-y. Learn both, but lean on the short form when you speak.

Reusable speaking chunks say these without translating

Guten Morgen! Guten Tag! Guten Abend! Hallo! Wie geht's? Gut, danke. Und dir? / Und Ihnen? Ein bisschen. Bis später! Bis bald! Auf Wiedersehen! Tschüss! Wie bitte? Ich verstehe nicht. Können Sie das wiederholen? Wie schreibt man das?

Classroom survival phrases

Wie bitte? ("sorry, what?") and Ich verstehe nicht ("I don't understand") are what every teacher expects you to use instead of switching to English. Use them constantly — they mark you as a serious student, not a lazy one.

AussprachePronunciation

Only the sounds this chapter actually needs — the ones that trip up English speakers on greetings and names.

ch
Two different sounds depending on the letter before it. After i, e: a soft "hiss," tongue near the roof of the mouth (like the start of "huge" said gently). After a, o, u: a rougher back-of-throat sound.
ich (soft) · Nachname (rough) · Buchstabe (rough)
ei
Sounds like English "eye," not "ay." This trips people up constantly because it looks like it should sound like "ei" in "weigh."
heißen · Beruf · nein
ie
A long "ee" sound, like in "see." Opposite of ei above — easy to mix the two up.
Sie · wie · sieben
z
Always pronounced "ts," never like English "z" (as in "zoo"). This is the single most common beginner error in this chapter.
zwei · zehn · zwanzig
w
Pronounced like English "v," not "w."
wie · woher · wohnen
v
Usually pronounced like English "f" in native German words.
Vorname · von

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Heute ich bin müde.
Heute bin ich müde.
Why: German always keeps the conjugated verb in position two. When a time word starts the sentence, the subject has to move after the verb — English word order does not transfer directly.
Du bin aus Berlin.
Du bist aus Berlin.
Why: sein is irregular and each pronoun has its own form. "Bin" only goes with "ich" — never mix and match.
Woher wohnst du?
Wo wohnst du?
Why: woher asks about origin (where from), wo asks about current location (where). "Living from" somewhere doesn't make sense in either language.
du (to a stranger or your teacher)
Sie (to a stranger or your teacher)
Why: formality isn't optional politeness in German the way it can feel in English — using du with the wrong person reads as disrespectful or presumptuous.
"z" pronounced like English "zoo"
"z" pronounced "ts," as in "cats"
Why: German z is always a ts-sound. Saying "zwei" with an English z is one of the most immediate tells of an untrained ear.

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

The final A1 chapter. It rounds off your question-word toolkit (adding wen and wem, the accusative and dative forms of "who") and introduces the impersonal pronoun man — genuinely one of the most useful small words in German.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn vacation types and destinations, weather vocabulary (building on Kapitel 6), compass directions, the impersonal pronoun man ("one/you," used generically), and the complete set of question pronouns: wer, wen, wem, was.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Talk about vacation plans, past trips, and travel preferences
  • Describe weather across different regions
  • Use man to make general statements ("you can..." / "one does...")
  • Ask "who" in every grammatical role: subject, direct object, indirect object
  • Write a simple postcard or describe a trip you took

Where you use this in real life

Planning your Bali trip in German for practice, describing a past holiday, writing a postcard, or making general statements about what people typically do somewhere ("man spricht hier Deutsch" — "German is spoken here").

GrammatikGrammar

Two final building blocks: the impersonal pronoun man, and the complete wer/wen/wem/was question paradigm.

1. The impersonal pronoun man "one/you/people in general"

man is used to make a general statement without naming who specifically — similar to English "you" or "one" in "you can see the sea from here," where "you" doesn't mean the listener specifically. It's conjugated exactly like er/sie/es (third person singular).

✓
Man kann hier gut schwimmen.
You can swim well here. (generally, anyone can)
✓
Hier spricht man Deutsch.
German is spoken here. (people in general speak it)
✕
Man spreche Deutsch.
Wrong — man conjugates normally like er/sie/es, not as some special form

Memory trick

Never confuse man (the pronoun "one/you") with der Mann (the noun "man/husband") — they sound similar but are completely unrelated grammatically. man is lowercase and never takes an article.

2. The complete wer-family who, in every case

You've used wer (nominative "who") since Kapitel 2. This chapter completes the set: wen (accusative "whom") and wem (dative "to whom").

CaseWordAsks about
Nominativewerthe subject — "who does X?"
Accusativewenthe direct object — "who(m) do you see?"
Dativewemthe indirect object — "to/for whom?"
✓
Wer kommt mit?
Who's coming along? (subject)
✓
Wen besuchst du?
Who(m) are you visiting? (direct object — besuchen takes accusative)
✓
Wem gibst du das Geschenk?
To whom are you giving the gift? (indirect object — dative)

Common mistake

English speakers often use "who" for everything ("who do you see?" is common even though formally it should be "whom"), which makes it easy to default to wer for every question in German too. Check the verb: does it take accusative (wen) or dative (wem)? helfen is dative, so "who are you helping" is Wem hilfst du?, not Wen hilfst du?

POSTKARTE
Liebe Sonia, hier ist es toll. Man kann gut schwimmen und wandern. Bis bald!
Dear Sonia, it's great here. You can swim and hike well. See you soon!

AussprachePronunciation

man vs. Mann
Pronounced identically, but never confuse them in writing — man is the pronoun (lowercase, no article), Mann is the noun "man/husband" (capitalized, takes der).
man kann · der Mann
ei (Meer — no, contrast: Urlaub has au)
Reinforcing the "ow" sound of au from earlier chapters, since Urlaub uses it.
Urlaub
st (Osten, Westen)
Mid-word st is pronounced plainly, as covered in Kapitel 4 — not the "sht" sound reserved for word-initial st.
Osten · Westen

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Man spreche Deutsch hier.
Man spricht hier Deutsch.
Why: man is conjugated exactly like er/sie/es in the normal present tense — there's no special subjunctive-like form needed.
Wen hilfst du?
Wem hilfst du?
Why: helfen is one of the fixed dative verbs from Kapitel 7, so its question word must be wem (dative), not wen (accusative).
Wir sind ans Meer gemacht.
Wir sind ans Meer gefahren.
Why: describing travel to a destination needs a motion verb like fahren, which also correctly triggers sein as the Perfekt helper — machen would need haben and doesn't fit the meaning.

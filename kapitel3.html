Places in a city, articles, plurals, and asking for directions

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 3 takes you outside — naming what you see in a city and asking a stranger how to get somewhere. It also introduces the indefinite article properly (ein/eine) and its negative twin (kein/keine), which you'll use every single day.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn to name buildings and places in a city, use ein/eine ("a/an") and kein/keine ("not a / no"), form plurals (you've been seeing them, now you learn the patterns), ask for and understand directions, and give simple commands using the imperative — the polite "Sie" command form used constantly for instructions.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Name common city buildings and places: town hall, station, church, bridge, park, cinema
  • Say "there is a..." and "there is no..." for places and objects
  • Ask politely for directions and understand basic direction instructions
  • Use command forms: "go straight," "turn left," "take the bus"
  • Recognize and form plurals for nouns you already know

Where you use this in real life

Finding your way around an unfamiliar German city, asking a passerby for directions to the Bahnhof, understanding a receptionist's instructions ("Gehen Sie geradeaus, dann links"), or describing your own neighborhood in Noida to someone in German.

GrammatikGrammar

Three big ideas: indefinite/negative articles, plural patterns, and the imperative (command form).

1. Indefinite article ein/eine "a/an"

German nouns have grammatical gender: masculine (der), feminine (die), neuter (das). The indefinite article changes to match — but only two forms exist at this stage: ein for masculine and neuter, eine for feminine.

der/das noun → ein + noun
die noun → eine + noun
Definite (the)Indefinite (a/an)English
der Bahnhofein Bahnhofa station
das Hotelein Hotela hotel
die Kircheeine Kirchea church
✓
Das ist ein Park.
That's a park.
✓
Das ist eine Brücke.
That's a bridge.
✕
Das ist ein Brücke.
Wrong — Brücke is feminine (die), so it needs eine

Memory trick

Always learn a new noun together with its article — say "die Brücke," never just "Brücke." Trying to guess gender from the English meaning fails constantly; German gender is often arbitrary to an English speaker's ear.

2. Negative article kein/keine "not a / no"

kein negates a noun that has (or could have) an indefinite article. It follows the exact same der/das → kein, die → keine pattern as ein.

kein + [der/das-noun] · keine + [die-noun]
✓
Das ist kein Hotel.
That's not a hotel.
✓
Das ist keine Kirche, das ist ein Museum.
That's not a church, that's a museum.
✕
Das ist nicht ein Hotel.
Wrong (or at least unnatural) — English speakers reach for nicht + ein out of habit, but German merges them into kein

Common mistake

English negates with "not" and leaves the article alone ("that is not a hotel"). German fuses the negation into the article itself: nicht + ein → kein. Using nicht ein is a dead giveaway of English-brain translation.

3. Plural forms no single rule — but recognizable patterns

German plurals don't follow one universal rule the way English "-s" mostly does. Instead there are several patterns, and you learn each noun's plural as part of vocabulary. The good news: patterns cluster, so you start recognizing them.

PatternSingularPlural
-eder Parkdie Parks
-e (+ umlaut)der Turmdie Türme
-er (+ umlaut)das Hausdie Häuser
-endie Kirchedie Kirchen
-sdas Hoteldie Hotels
no changeder Bahnhofdie Bahnhöfe

One rule that always holds

Whatever the plural pattern, the plural article is always die — regardless of the singular's gender. das Hotel → die Hotels, der Turm → die Türme. This is the one shortcut you can rely on completely.

4. The imperative giving commands and instructions — Sie-form

To tell someone (formally) to do something, take the Sie-form of the verb and simply swap the order: verb first, then Sie.

VERB + Sie + (rest)!
✓
Gehen Sie geradeaus!
Go straight ahead!
✓
Nehmen Sie den Bus!
Take the bus!
✕
Sie gehen geradeaus!
Not a command — this is just a statement ("you go straight") with an exclamation mark

Memory trick

The Sie-imperative looks exactly like a yes/no question except it ends in "!" instead of "?" — Gehen Sie geradeaus? (question) vs. Gehen Sie geradeaus! (command). Same word order, different punctuation and intonation.

5. Asking politely for directions

Entschuldigung, wo ist [der/die/das + place]?
✓
Entschuldigung, wo ist der Bahnhof?
Excuse me, where is the station?
✓
Entschuldigung, wo ist hier eine Bank?
Excuse me, is there a bank around here?

AMAN

REZEPTION

AMAN
Ist das weit?
Is that far?
REZEPTION

No, that's not far. Five minutes on foot.

AussprachePronunciation

ü
Round your lips as for "u," but say "i." Appears in many plurals with umlaut, so getting it right matters constantly.
Brücke · Türme · Züge
sch
Like English "sh" in "shoe."
Schiff · geschehen
s+p / s+t
At the start of a word or syllable, s before p or t is pronounced "sh" — Straße sounds like "Shtrasse," not "Strasse."
Supermarkt (soo-per-markt — note: not word-initial st, so normal s) · Straße
au
Like English "ow" in "how."
Rathaus · geradeaus · Auto

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Das ist nicht ein Hotel.
Das ist kein Hotel.
Why: German fuses nicht + ein into the single word kein — you never say them side by side.
Das ist ein Brücke.
Das ist eine Brücke.
Why: Brücke is a die-noun (feminine), so the indefinite article must be eine, not ein.
Sie gehen geradeaus!
Gehen Sie geradeaus!
Why: the Sie-imperative always puts the verb first — leaving Sie in front makes it a statement, not a command.
die Hotel
die Hotels
Why: das Hotel becomes die Hotels in the plural — the plural article is always die, but the noun itself still needs its plural ending, which is easy to forget.

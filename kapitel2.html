KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 1 got you through the first 30 seconds of a conversation. Kapitel 2 gives you the next five minutes: talking about what you like doing, your daily activities, your job, and the people in your life — friends and colleagues.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn regular present-tense verb conjugation properly (this is the pattern almost every German verb follows), how to say what you like or don't like doing with gern, the days of the week, numbers above 20, talking about jobs and working hours, and filling out a simple form with your personal details.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Say what you like doing and what you don't like doing
  • Ask a friend or colleague about their hobbies and daily routine
  • Name all seven days of the week and say what you do on which day
  • Talk about your job, where you work, and your working hours
  • Count above 20 and give a full phone number or age
  • Fill out a basic form (name, address, phone, job) and understand one when handed to you

Where you use this in real life

Small talk with a colleague at BRPL, chatting with a classmate about weekend plans, describing your job at a networking event, or filling in a registration form at a Volkshochschule (adult education center) — this chapter is the vocabulary of ordinary weekday life.

GrammatikGrammar

The big one here is regular verb conjugation — once you have this pattern, you can use hundreds of German verbs correctly on day one of learning them.

1. Regular verb conjugation the pattern nearly every verb follows

German verbs in the dictionary end in -en (occasionally -n) — this is the infinitive, the "to ___" form. To use a verb with a real subject, you remove the ending and add a new one that matches the subject.

stem (verb minus -en) + ending
ich -e · du -st · er/sie/es -t · wir -en · ihr -t · sie/Sie -en
Pronounhören (to hear/listen)spielen (to play)arbeiten (to work)
ichhörespielearbeite
duhörstspielstarbeitest
er/sie/eshörtspieltarbeitet
wirhörenspielenarbeiten
ihrhörtspieltarbeitet
sie/Siehörenspielenarbeiten

Common mistake

Verbs whose stem ends in -t or -d (like arbeiten) insert an extra -e- before the du/er/ihr endings — arbeitest, not arbeitst. This is purely for pronunciation: try saying "arbeitst" fast and you'll hear why German inserts the vowel.

Memory trick

Three pronouns always share the infinitive-looking form: wir, sie (they), Sie all just use the full -en ending — no change from the dictionary form at all.

2. Stem-changing verbs a small irregular group you'll meet constantly

A handful of very common verbs change their vowel in the du/er/sie forms only. You already met one in Kapitel 1: sprechen. Here's another important one.

Pronounlesen (to read) — e→ie
ichlese
duliest
er/sie/esliest
wirlesen

Learn later

The full logic of vowel-changing verbs (a→ä, e→i, e→ie) is covered properly in a later chapter. For now, just memorize sprechen and lesen as irregular du/er forms — don't try to derive the pattern yet.

3. gern — saying you like doing something

German has no separate verb "to like doing" for activities — you just add the word gern (gladly) after the verb. It's simple once you see it, but English speakers often try to translate "like" directly and get stuck.

subject + VERB + gern + (rest)
✓
Ich höre gern Musik.
I like listening to music. (literally: I listen gladly to music)
✓
Sonia spielt gern Tennis.
Sonia likes playing tennis.
✕
Ich mag hören Musik.
Wrong — this mixes English "like" logic into German incorrectly

To say you like something a lot, use sehr gern. To say you don't like it, add nicht before gern: nicht so gern (not really).

✓
Ich koche sehr gern.
I really like cooking.
✓
Ich schwimme nicht so gern.
I don't really like swimming.

Memory trick

gern parks itself right after the verb, almost every time. Say the verb, then immediately say gern — train your mouth to do this as one unit: "spiel-gern," "koch-gern," "les-gern."

4. Verb mögen and möchten liking things (nouns) and wanting things

gern works with verbs (activities). For nouns (things you like), German uses the verb mögen. Its close cousin möchten means "would like" — used for wanting/ordering something specific.

Pronounmögen (to like)möchten (would like)
ichmagmöchte
dumagstmöchtest
er/sie/esmagmöchte
✓
Ich mag Kaffee.
I like coffee.
✓
Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte.
I'd like a coffee, please.

Common mistake

Ich mag lesen instead of Ich lese gern — both are technically heard, but A1 courses expect gern for activities and mögen for things/nouns. Keep them separate for now.

5. Time expressions in position one days of the week move the verb

Just like in Kapitel 1, when you start a sentence with a time expression (a day, "today," "tomorrow"), the verb stays in position two and the subject slides after it.

Am Montagpos. 1
arbeiteverb — pos. 2
ich.subject moves
✓
Am Montag arbeite ich.
On Monday I work.
✕
Am Montag ich arbeite.
Wrong — verb has drifted to position three, same mistake as Kapitel 1

6. Ja-/Nein-Frage yes/no questions — verb jumps to position 1

For a yes/no question, there's no question word at all — the verb simply moves to position 1, and the subject follows in position 2.

VERB + subject + rest?
Gehenverb — pos. 1
wirsubject — pos. 2
ins Kino?rest
✓
Gehen wir ins Kino?
Shall we go to the cinema?
✓
Spielst du gern Fußball?
Do you like playing football?

Three sentence types, one shared rule: statement (subject+verb), W-Frage (question word+verb+subject), Ja/Nein-Frage (verb+subject) — the conjugated verb is always in position 1 or 2, never buried later.

7. Doch the special "yes" that contradicts a negative question

A plain Ja/Nein becomes ambiguous after a negative question. German uses doch specifically to mean "yes, actually I do" — contradicting the negative.

✓
Trinkst du nicht gern Kaffee? — Doch, ich trinke gern Kaffee.
Don't you like coffee? — Yes, actually I do.
✓
Trinkst du nicht gern Kaffee? — Nein, ich trinke Kaffee nicht gern.
Don't you like coffee? — No, I really don't.

Common mistake

doch only appears after a negative question, as a contradiction. For an ordinary positive question, just use Ja/Nein as normal — doch never appears there.

Kapitel 1+2 ÜbungenCombined practice — every person, every pattern

Everything from Kapitel 1 and 2 together — sentence framing across ich/du/er/sie/wir/ihr/sie(Sie), and rewriting a bio between first and third person. This is the real test of whether the grammar has actually stuck, not just whether you can recognize it.

A. Person-conjugation drill same sentence frame, every pronoun

Take one sentence frame and build it correctly for all seven persons. This forces you to actually recall each ending instead of pattern-matching visually.

Frame 1 — kommen + aus + country (Kapitel 1)

Build: "[subject] + kommen (conjugated) + aus + [Land]"

Ich komme aus Indien. · Du kommst aus Deutschland. · Er/Sie kommt aus der Schweiz. · Wir kommen aus Japan. · Ihr kommt aus Frankreich. · Sie (they) kommen aus den USA. · Sie (formal) kommen aus Italien.

Watch the article exceptions: der Schweiz, den USA. Everyone else takes no article.

Frame 2 — gern + hobby verb (Kapitel 2)

Build: "[subject] + [hobby verb, conjugated] + gern"

Ich schwimme gern. · Du tanzt gern. · Er/Sie liest gern. · Wir joggen gern. · Ihr kocht gern. · Sie (they) reisen gern. · Sie (formal) fotografieren gern.

lesen is irregular for du/er-sie (liest) — everything else follows the regular -e/-st/-t/-en pattern.

Frame 3 — haben + frei + day (Kapitel 2)

Build: "[subject] + haben (conjugated) + am [Tag] + frei"

Ich habe am Montag frei. · Du hast am Dienstag frei. · Er/Sie hat am Mittwoch frei. · Wir haben am Donnerstag frei. · Ihr habt am Freitag frei. · Sie (they) haben am Samstag frei. · Sie (formal) haben am Sonntag frei.

haben drops the "b" for du/er-sie: hast, hat — not habst/habt.

Frame 4 — sein + Beruf (Kapitel 1 + 2)

Build: "[subject] + sein (conjugated) + [Beruf]"

Ich bin Ärztin. · Du bist Ingenieur. · Er/Sie ist Lehrerin. · Wir sind Studenten. · Ihr seid Kellner. · Sie (they) sind Architektinnen. · Sie (formal) sind Friseurin.

sein is fully irregular — bin, bist, ist, sind, seid, sind. No pattern to derive, just memorized.

B. Paragraph rewriting first person → third person

Write a short bio about yourself, then rewrite the exact same information about someone else — switching every verb ending and pronoun. This is the actual skill behind Kapitel 1's "Personalpronomen in Texten" and Kapitel 2's job/hobby vocabulary working together.

Exercise 1 — rewrite this first-person bio in third person (using "er")

"Ich heiße Jan. Ich komme aus Frankfurt und wohne in Zürich. Ich spreche Deutsch und Englisch. Ich bin Ingenieur von Beruf. Ich arbeite von Montag bis Freitag. Ich habe am Wochenende frei. Ich schwimme gern und ich lese gern."

Das ist Jan. Er kommt aus Frankfurt und wohnt in Zürich. Er spricht Deutsch und Englisch. Er ist Ingenieur von Beruf. Er arbeitet von Montag bis Freitag. Er hat am Wochenende frei. Er schwimmt gern und er liest gern.

Every verb picks up the er/sie ending: komme→kommt, wohne→wohnt, spreche→spricht, bin→ist, arbeite→arbeitet, habe→hat, schwimme→schwimmt, lese→liest (irregular stem stays).

Exercise 2 — rewrite this bio using "sie" (she) instead of the name

"Magda Donat kommt aus Polen. Magda Donat wohnt in Berlin. Magda Donat ist Ärztin. Magda Donat arbeitet auch nachts und am Wochenende. Magda Donat spielt gern Tennis."

Das ist Magda Donat. Sie kommt aus Polen. Sie wohnt in Berlin. Sie ist Ärztin. Sie arbeitet auch nachts und am Wochenende. Sie spielt gern Tennis.

Name the person once, then switch to sie for every sentence after — never repeat the full name mid-paragraph.

C. Your turn — write your own paragraph

Using both chapters' vocabulary, write 4–5 sentences about yourself: name, origin, residence, languages, profession, working days, and one hobby with gern. Then rewrite the same paragraph in third person, as if introducing yourself to a class.

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Ich mag hören Musik.
Ich höre gern Musik.
Why: gern + verb is the A1 pattern for liking an activity. mögen is reserved for liking a thing (a noun), not an action.
Du arbeitst bei einer Firma.
Du arbeitest bei einer Firma.
Why: verb stems ending in -t or -d need an extra -e- before -st/-t endings for pronunciation reasons.
Am Montag ich arbeite.
Am Montag arbeite ich.
Why: verb-position-two rule again — a fronted time expression pushes the subject after the verb.
Ich spiele Fußball gern.
Ich spiele gern Fußball.
Why: gern sits directly after the verb, before the object, almost always — putting it at the end sounds unnatural to a native ear even though it's not strictly ungrammatical in every case.

Meine Wohnung — My apartment

Rooms, furniture, and two-way prepositions (wo vs. wohin)

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 9 delivers the trickiest grammar point at A1: two-way prepositions that switch between dative and accusative depending on whether you're describing a location or a movement toward it. Once this clicks, a huge amount of German suddenly makes sense.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn rooms, furniture, and colors, how to describe an apartment, and the nine two-way prepositions (in, an, auf, unter, über, vor, hinter, neben, zwischen) that take dative for a static location (wo?) and accusative for movement toward a place (wohin?).

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Name rooms and furniture in an apartment
  • Describe where things are located, and where you're putting them
  • Distinguish wo (where — static) from wohin (where to — movement)
  • Describe an apartment using adjectives (bright, cozy, expensive, loud)
  • Express liking or disliking something about a place

Where you use this in real life

Describing your apartment in Noida, telling someone where to put something when they visit, understanding an apartment listing, or explaining what you like or dislike about your home.

GrammatikGrammar

This chapter's grammar is genuinely the hardest yet: the same preposition can mean two different things depending on the case that follows it.

1. Two-way prepositions Wechselpräpositionen — the core idea

Nine prepositions can take either dative or accusative. The choice isn't random — it depends on the question being answered: wo? (where — a fixed location) takes dative; wohin? (where to — movement toward a place) takes accusative.

wo? (location, no movement) → DATIVE
wohin? (movement toward) → ACCUSATIVE

2. Location (wo?) — dative example

✓
Das Sofa steht im Wohnzimmer.
The sofa is in the living room. (in + dem → im; already there, no movement)
✓
Die Lampe steht neben dem Bett.
The lamp is next to the bed. (dem = dative, masculine/neuter)
✓
Der Teppich liegt auf dem Boden.
The rug lies on the floor.

3. Movement (wohin?) — accusative example

✓
Ich stelle das Sofa ins Wohnzimmer.
I'm putting the sofa into the living room. (in + das → ins; movement into the room)
✓
Ich stelle die Lampe neben das Bett.
I'm putting the lamp next to the bed. (das = accusative, neuter)
✕
Ich stelle die Lampe neben dem Bett.
Wrong — this is movement (placing something), so it needs accusative dem→das, not dative dem

Common mistake

English doesn't mark this distinction at all — "the lamp is next to the bed" and "I put the lamp next to the bed" both use "the" unchanged. This makes the wo/wohin split invisible to English speakers unless they actively check: is something moving into position, or already sitting still?

Memory trick

Ask yourself: is there a verb of motion (stellen, legen, hängen — "to put/place/hang") aimed at a destination? → accusative. Is there a verb of rest (stehen, liegen, hängen — "to stand/lie/hang," describing where something already is) → dative. Note hängen can be either, depending on whether it's the "hanging something up" action or "already hanging" state.

AussprachePronunciation

long vs. short e
German distinguishes clearly between a long, tense "e" (as in Regal) and a short, relaxed "e" (as in Bett) — the vowel length actually changes meaning in some word pairs, so listen carefully.
Regal (long e) · Bett (short e)
sch (Schrank)
"Sh" sound, as covered before — reinforcing since furniture vocabulary uses it often.
Schrank · Tisch
ü (gemütlich)
Rounded lips + "i" — appears in one of this chapter's most useful adjectives.
gemütlich

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Ich stelle das Sofa im Wohnzimmer.
Ich stelle das Sofa ins Wohnzimmer.
Why: stellen describes the act of placing something (movement toward a destination), so the two-way preposition needs accusative — ins, not im.
Das Bild hängt über den Bett.
Das Bild hängt über dem Bett.
Why: this describes where the picture already is (a static location, wo?), so it takes dative — dem, not accusative den.
Die Lampe steht neben den Sofa.
Die Lampe steht neben dem Sofa.
Why: stehen (to stand/be located) always pairs with dative for the location it's describing.

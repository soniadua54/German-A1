Alltag und Familie — Everyday life and family

Daily routines, telling time, family, possessives, and modal verbs

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

This is the densest chapter so far — three real grammar systems land at once: telling time, possessive articles (my/your/his/her), and modal verbs (can/must/want). Take it in pieces; don't expect to absorb all three in one sitting.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn to tell the time both formally (24-hour, for schedules) and informally (12-hour, for daily speech), describe your daily routine using separable verbs, talk about your family using possessive articles, and use the three core modal verbs — müssen (must), können (can), wollen (want to) — which introduce a completely new sentence shape.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Tell the time and say when something happens
  • Describe your daily routine from waking up to going to bed
  • Name your family members and say whose is whose using possessives
  • Say what you must, can, or want to do
  • Make an appointment or apologize for being late

Where you use this in real life

Scheduling a meeting, describing your workday, introducing your family to Sonia's German relatives, explaining you can't come because you must work, or apologizing when you're running late — this is the vocabulary of ordinary responsibility and routine.

GrammatikGrammar

Three systems: time expressions, possessive articles, and modal verbs with the verb bracket. Take each on its own — don't try to combine all three in a sentence yet.

1. Telling time two systems: formal and everyday

Germans use the 24-hour clock for anything official (train times, appointments, TV schedules) and a 12-hour conversational system in everyday speech — similar to how English says "half past three" instead of "15:30."

15:30fünfzehn Uhr dreißighalb vier

Common mistake

halb vier means "half PAST three" (3:30), not "half TO four" — this trips up English speakers constantly because "half four" in British English colloquially can mean 4:30. In German, halb always looks forward to the next hour, so halb vier = 3:30, always.

Wie spät ist es? / Wie viel Uhr ist es? — Es ist ...
✓
Wie spät ist es? — Es ist halb neun.
What time is it? — It's 8:30.
✓
Um wie viel Uhr beginnt der Kurs? — Um neun Uhr.
What time does the course start? — At nine o'clock.

3. Separable verbs a verb that splits into two parts

Some German verbs have a prefix that detaches and moves to the very end of the sentence in normal statements — the verb "splits" while the conjugated part stays in position two. aufstehen (to get up) is a classic example: auf- is the prefix.

subject + VERB-stem + (rest) + ...prefix.
Ichsubject
steheverb — pos. 2
um sieben Uhrmiddle
auf.prefix — end
✓
Ich stehe um sieben Uhr auf.
I get up at seven o'clock. (aufstehen splits)
✕
Ich aufstehe um sieben Uhr.
Wrong — the prefix must detach and move to the end, not stay glued to the verb

Memory trick

Think of the sentence as a sandwich: the conjugated verb-stem is the front slice of bread (position two), and the prefix is the back slice (end of sentence) — everything else is the filling in between. This "verb bracket" shape (Satzklammer) will reappear with modal verbs below.

4. Possessive articles mein, dein, sein, ihr...

Possessives follow the exact same der/die/das → ein/eine pattern you already know — they behave grammatically just like ein.

PronounPossessiveMeaning

sieihr/ihreher
wirunser/unsereour
SieIhr/Ihreyour (formal)
✓
Das ist mein Bruder.
That's my brother. (der Bruder → mein, like ein)
✓
Das ist meine Schwester.
That's my sister. (die Schwester → meine, like eine)
✓
Ich mag meinen Bruder.
I like my brother. (accusative: mein → meinen, masculine)

The accusative rule still applies

Possessives take the same accusative change as ein: masculine mein → meinen when it's the object. Er mag seinen Beruf ("he likes his job") — Beruf is masculine and it's the object, so sein → seinen.

5. Modal verbs: müssen, können, wollen must, can, want to

Modal verbs are irregular in the singular and introduce a brand-new sentence shape: the modal verb takes position two (as usual), but the main verb gets pushed to the infinitive form at the very end of the sentence.

Pronounmüssenkönnenwollen
ichmusskannwill

Ichsubject
mussmodal — pos. 2

✓
Ich muss heute arbeiten.
I have to work today.
✓
Kannst du morgen kommen?
Can you come tomorrow?
✓
Ich will einen Kaffee trinken.
I want to drink a coffee.
✕

Wrong — the infinitive must go all the way to the end, not stay next to the modal

Common mistake

English keeps the verb right after the modal: "I must work today." German pulls the second verb all the way to the end of the sentence, no matter how much sits between them: Ich muss heute noch bis sechs Uhr arbeiten — "arbeiten" still waits until the very end.

Memory trick

This is the same "verb bracket" (Satzklammer) shape as separable verbs: something in position two, something else parked at the end, everything else squeezed in the middle. Once you see this shape once, you'll recognize it everywhere in German.

AussprachePronunciation

-e (final)
A final unstressed -e is pronounced as a short, weak "uh" sound, never silent as it often is in English.
Familie · Tochter (final -er, similar principle) · meine
v (in Vater)
Pronounced "f," as covered in Kapitel 1 — worth reinforcing since Vater is one of the most common words in this chapter.
Vater
müssen / können
Both contain ü — round your lips for "u," say "i." Don't let it collapse into a plain "u" sound.
müssen · können (ö, not ü — round lips, say "e")

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Ich muss arbeiten heute.
Ich muss heute arbeiten.
Why: with modal verbs, the infinitive always goes to the very end of the sentence, no matter what else is in between.
Ich aufstehe um sieben.
Ich stehe um sieben auf.
Why: separable verbs split apart in a normal statement — the prefix detaches and moves to the end.
halb vier = 4:30
halb vier = 3:30
Why: halb always looks forward to the coming hour, not back to the one just passed — this is the opposite of how some English speakers use "half four" colloquially.
Ich mag mein Beruf.
Ich mag meinen Beruf.
Why: der Beruf is masculine and it's the object here, so mein must become meinen in the accusative — possessives follow the same rule as ein.

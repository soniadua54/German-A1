Guten Appetit! — Enjoy your meal!

Food, drinks, shopping — and the accusative case

KapitelüberblickChapter overview

Kapitel 4 introduces the accusative case — the first real German grammar hurdle. It changes how articles behave the moment a noun becomes the object of a sentence, and it shows up everywhere once you start talking about eating, buying, and wanting things.

What this chapter is about

You'll learn food and drink vocabulary, the names of different shops, how to talk about meals and mealtimes, and — most importantly — the accusative case: how "the/a" changes when a noun is the direct object of a verb like essen, kaufen, brauchen, möchten.

What you should be able to say after finishing it

  • Name common foods, drinks, and meals
  • Say what you eat, buy, need, or would like, using correct accusative articles
  • Have a basic shopping conversation at a bakery, butcher, or supermarket
  • Order food or drink politely
  • Say "Guten Appetit" and respond to it correctly

Where you use this in real life

Ordering in a café, shopping for groceries, telling a host what you'd like to eat, or explaining your diet — this chapter's grammar (accusative) will keep appearing in almost every future chapter, so it's worth getting solid here.

GrammatikGrammar

The accusative case marks the direct object of a sentence — the thing being eaten, bought, or wanted. Only the masculine article actually changes; feminine, neuter, and plural stay the same as nominative. This makes it more manageable than it sounds.

1. What "case" means the role a noun plays in the sentence

In English, word order tells you who does what: "I eat the apple" — "I" is the doer (subject), "the apple" is the thing being acted on (object). German uses word order too, but it also changes the article to mark this role explicitly. The subject's case is called nominative (which you already know — der/die/das). The direct object's case is called accusative.

2. The accusative article change only masculine changes

der → den (masculine only)
die → die (no change)
das → das (no change)
die (plural) → die (no change)
GenderNominative (subject)Accusative (object)
masculineder Kuchenden Kuchen
femininedie Wurstdie Wurst
neuterdas Brotdas Brot
pluraldie Eierdie Eier
✓
Ich esse den Kuchen.
I eat the cake. (der Kuchen → den Kuchen, because it's the object)
✓
Ich kaufe die Wurst.
I buy the sausage. (die stays die)
✕
Ich esse der Kuchen.
Wrong — der must become den when it's the object of essen

Memory trick

Only masculine changes, and only in one direction: der → den. Feminine, neuter, and plural nouns are "invisible" to the accusative — they look exactly the same as nominative. This means you only have to actively remember one change, not four.

3. Indefinite article in the accusative ein → einen

The same pattern applies to ein: masculine ein becomes einen in the accusative. Feminine eine and neuter ein stay unchanged.

GenderNominativeAccusative
masculineein Apfeleinen Apfel
feminineeine Bananeeine Banane
neuterein Brotein Brot
✓
Ich möchte einen Apfel.
I'd like an apple.
✓
Ich brauche eine Gurke.
I need a cucumber.

The same happens to kein: kein → keinen for masculine.

✓
Ich habe keinen Käse mehr.
I don't have any more cheese.

4. Verbs that take an accusative object the verbs that trigger this whole chapter

VerbMeaningExample
essento eatIch esse den Salat.
trinkento drinkIch trinke den Kaffee.
kaufento buyIch kaufe das Brot.
brauchento needIch brauche den Zucker.
möchtenwould likeIch möchte den Kuchen.
nehmento takeIch nehme den Fisch.

Common mistake

English speakers often skip the article change because English doesn't do this at all — "I eat the apple" never changes "the" no matter its grammatical role. In German, forgetting to change der → den after these verbs is one of the most common A1 errors, and it's the one teachers correct most.

5. essen — irregular, stem-changing verb

PronounForm
ichesse
duisst
er/sie/esisst
wiressen

Watch the spelling

du isst and er/sie isst are identical — this is a coincidence of German spelling rules (the stem already ends in -ss, so the -t ending merges visually). Don't second-guess it; it's correct as written.

SprechfabrikSpeaking factory

Dialogue 1 — At the bakery

VERKÄUFERIN
Guten Tag! Was möchten Sie?
Good day! What would you like?
AMAN
Ich möchte eine Gurke, einen Salat und acht Brötchen.
I'd like a cucumber, a lettuce, and eight rolls.
VERKÄUFERIN
Tut mir leid, ich habe keinen Salat mehr.
Sorry, I don't have any more lettuce.
AMAN
Oh. Dann nehme ich eine Packung Nudeln und ein Stück Schinken.
Oh. Then I'll take a packet of pasta and a piece of ham.
VERKÄUFERIN
Bitte. Haben Sie sonst noch einen Wunsch?
Here you go. Anything else?
AMAN
Nein, danke. Das ist alles.
No, thanks. That's all.

Dialogue 2 — At the dinner table

SONIA
Guten Appetit!
Enjoy your meal!
AMAN
Danke, gleichfalls!
Thanks, you too!
SONIA
Möchtest du noch ein Würstchen?
Would you like another sausage?
AMAN
Ja, gerne. Danke.
Yes, gladly. Thanks.

Dialogue 3 — In a café

KELLNER
Was trinken Sie?
What are you drinking?
AMAN
Ich möchte einen Milchkaffee, bitte.
I'd like a latte, please.
KELLNER
Und etwas essen?
And something to eat?
AMAN
Ja, einen Kuchen, bitte.
Yes, a piece of cake, please.

AussprachePronunciation

st
In the middle of a word (not word-initial), s+t is a normal English-style "st," not "sh-t."
isst · kostet
ä
Like the "e" in English "bed," slightly more open.
Äpfel · Käse (long ä-sound in Käse is actually "ä" spelled with umlaut on a, but pronounced like a long version)
eu / äu
Both spelled differently but pronounced the same: like "oy" in English "boy."
Bäckerei (no eu here, but note the ei = "eye") · neu · Häuser
g (final)
At the end of a word, g often softens toward a "k" sound.
Tag (sounds close to "Tak")

Häufige FehlerCommon mistakes

Ich esse der Kuchen.
Ich esse den Kuchen.
Why: der Kuchen is the direct object of essen, so it must take the accusative form den — English never marks this change, which is why it's so easy to forget.
Ich möchte ein Kaffee.
Ich möchte einen Kaffee.
Why: der Kaffee is masculine, so ein becomes einen in the accusative.
Ich habe kein Käse mehr.
Ich habe keinen Käse mehr.
Why: same accusative rule applies to kein — der Käse is masculine, so kein becomes keinen.
Ich mag essen Pizza.
Ich esse gern Pizza.
Why: as in Kapitel 2, use gern + conjugated verb for activities, not mögen + infinitive.
